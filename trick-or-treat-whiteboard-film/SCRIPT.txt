You'd never guess “trick or treat” was first printed in 1927, in Canada.
So why do Americans still say it?
The idea is older than the phrase.
In Scotland and Ireland, kids dressed up and went door to door on Halloween, trading a song or a joke for a snack.
But by the 1920s, Halloween in North America had a prank problem.
Gates vanished. Windows got soaped.
So the deal became simple: give us a treat, or we play a trick.
One of the earliest known uses of “trick or treat” shows up in a 1927 newspaper from Alberta, Canada.
After World War Two, trick-or-treating spread across American neighborhoods and became the candy-filled tradition we know.
So every “trick or treat” is a tiny, polite threat.
Thankfully, most of us pay up.

Fact notes
- Earliest widely cited printed use of "trick or treat": the Lethbridge Herald, Alberta, Canada, November 1927 (an earlier 1917
  mention from Saskatchewan has since been reported, hence the script's "one of the earliest known uses").
- Guising / souling in Scotland and Ireland: children in costume went door to door, performing a song, poem or joke for food or coins.
- Early-20th-century North American Halloween was notorious for vandalism (gates removed, windows soaped); towns promoted organised
  treats partly to curb pranks.
- Trick-or-treating spread widely across the US after sugar rationing ended following World War Two (late 1940s-1950s).
