You've never noticed that a 1 dollar bill and a 100 are the exact same size.
But why does every bill look the same?
Let's start with the green.
In 1861, the Union needed cash for the Civil War and printed paper notes with green ink on the back.
People called them greenbacks.
Why green? Cameras back then only shot in black and white, so a green back was harder to copy.
The ink was also plentiful and tough, holding up against wear and chemicals.
Then the size. Bills used to be bigger.
In 1929, the government shrank them to cut printing costs, and gave every bill the same size and a uniform design.
And they kept the classic green.
So your wallet looks like a stack of twins on purpose.
Cheaper to print, harder to fake.

Fact notes
- US notes have all been the same size (6.14 x 2.61 in) since the small-size notes of 1929 (Series 1928, issued July 1929),
  which were about 30% smaller than the older "horse blanket" notes, a change made largely to cut paper and printing costs.
- 1861: the Treasury issued Demand Notes to pay for the Civil War; their green-inked backs earned the nickname "greenbacks".
- Green backs as an anti-counterfeiting measure against black-and-white photography, and the durability and availability of
  the green ink, are the explanations given by the Bureau of Engraving and Printing and the Federal Reserve, though the exact
  original reason is not fully documented.
