America votes on a Tuesday. Not a weekend, a Tuesday. Why?
Blame 1845. That's when Congress set one national Election Day, and most Americans were farmers.
Voting often meant a long buggy ride to the county seat, sometimes a full day each way.
Sunday was for church, so no travel then.
Wednesday was market day in many towns.
That left Tuesday: travel Monday, vote Tuesday, back for market.
Why November? The harvest was in, and winter hadn't hit the roads yet.
The horse and buggy is long gone, but the Tuesday stuck.

Fact notes
- An Act of Congress of January 23, 1845 set a single date for choosing presidential electors: "the Tuesday next after the first
  Monday in the month of November". Congress later applied the same day to House (1872) and Senate (1914) elections.
- The usual explanation, cited by the National Archives and others: the US was largely rural, many voters travelled a day or more
  to the county seat; Sunday was a day of worship and Wednesday was a market day in many towns, so Tuesday allowed travel on
  Monday. November fell after the harvest and before harsh winter travel. (The rule's wording also kept Election Day off November 1.)
