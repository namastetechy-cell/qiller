Why is almost every American pencil yellow, with a number two on the side?
First, there's no lead inside. There never was.
It's graphite, mixed with clay.
In the 1500s, English miners found graphite and thought it was lead. The name stuck.
The number tells you the mix.
More clay: harder and lighter. Less clay: softer and darker.
Number two sits right in the middle: dark enough to read, hard enough not to smudge.
That's why test-scoring machines asked for it.
And the yellow? In the 1890s, a fancy pencil maker painted its best pencils yellow. Everyone copied.

Fact notes: pencil "lead" is graphite mixed with clay — there has never been lead in it; graphite found in Borrowdale, England, in the
1500s was taken for a form of lead ("plumbago"). More clay makes a harder, lighter core; less clay a softer, darker one. The US No. 2
(≈ HB) is the middle grade, and optical test-scoring sheets asked for No. 2 pencils because their marks are dark and don't smudge
easily. In the 1890s Koh-i-Noor Hardtmuth painted its premium pencils yellow; other makers copied the colour to signal quality.
