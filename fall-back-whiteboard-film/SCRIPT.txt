You'll gain one hour this Sunday, but you lost it in March and never noticed.
Why do we fall back at all?
At 2 a.m. Sunday, clocks jump back to 1 a.m. That's "fall back."
It's just returning the hour we borrowed in spring, when we "sprang forward."
So why borrow it? World War One.
In 1918, the U.S. adopted daylight saving time to shift daylight into the evening and save fuel for the war.
And the myth? It wasn't for farmers.
Many farmers hated it. Cows don't read clocks.
After decades of on and off, a 1966 law set nationwide rules.
Today it runs from March to the first Sunday in November.
Hawaii and most of Arizona skip it entirely.
So enjoy the extra hour of sleep. You earned it back.

Fact notes
- In 2026 US daylight saving time ends at 2:00 a.m. on Sunday, November 1 (clocks go back to 1:00 a.m.); it began on Sunday, March 8.
- The Standard Time Act of March 1918 brought daylight saving time to the US as a World War One fuel-saving measure; it was repealed
  for peacetime in 1919, returned as year-round "War Time" in 1942-45, and was otherwise a local patchwork.
- Farmers largely opposed daylight saving time (their work follows the sun, not the clock) and lobbied for the 1919 repeal.
- The Uniform Time Act of 1966 set national rules; since 2007 DST runs from the second Sunday in March to the first Sunday in November.
- Hawaii and Arizona do not observe DST, except the Navajo Nation in Arizona, which does (hence "most of Arizona").
