Why are school erasers pink? And what is that blue end actually for?
Blame a pencil company called Eberhard Faber.
In 1916, it launched the Pink Pearl eraser.
The story goes that its rubber was mixed with fine volcanic pumice from Italy, which gave it a pinkish tint.
It worked so well that pink simply became what an eraser looks like.
Now, that blue end. It's grittier and harder, made for ink and heavy marks.
But it doesn't really erase ink. It sands off the top layer of paper, ink and all.
That's why it tears holes in your homework.

Fact notes: Eberhard Faber introduced the Pink Pearl eraser in 1916. Its pink colour is commonly attributed to the fine Italian
volcanic pumice used as the abrasive (often-told company history; hence "the story goes"). The blue/grey end of two-tone erasers
is harder and more abrasive, sold for ink and heavy marks; it removes ink mainly by abrading the paper's surface, which is why it
can thin or tear the page.
