Your grandparents' Halloween bags didn't look like yours.
You'd never guess what changed after 1945.
So why candy?
Early door-to-door treats were whatever people had at home: fruit, nuts, homemade baked goods, sometimes a few coins.
Halloween also had a prank problem.
Handing out treats became a way to keep the peace with the neighborhood kids.
During World War Two, sugar was rationed.
Once rationing ended, candy companies started mass-producing Halloween candy, and trick-or-treating boomed.
Later, tampering scares pushed parents toward sealed, store-bought candy.
Wrapped meant safe.
So that bowl by your door is part peace deal, part postwar sugar rush.

Fact notes
- Early 20th-century "beggars' night" / guising treats were home goods: apples and other fruit, nuts, cookies, popcorn balls, coins.
- Organised treat-giving in the 1920s-30s was promoted partly to curb Halloween vandalism.
- US sugar rationing ran from 1942 to 1947; afterwards candy makers marketed Halloween candy heavily and trick-or-treating boomed
  in the postwar suburbs.
- Fears of tampered treats (razor blades, poison) grew in the 1970s-80s; actual cases were extremely rare, but parents and safety
  advice shifted to factory-sealed, store-bought candy.
