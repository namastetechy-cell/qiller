Stop blaming yourself for "one more video."
You're losing nights to three tricks built into your phone.
But which one gets you first?
Today we're unmasking all three, and at the end, I'll show you how to beat them.
Trick number one: the slot machine.
Back in the 1950s, psychologist B.F. Skinner found something wild.
If you reward behavior every single time, it gets boring.
But if you reward it randomly, only some of the time, the behavior sticks the hardest.
That's exactly how a slot machine works.
And that's exactly how your feed works.
Boring post.
Boring post.
Boring post.
Then, boom, a dog playing the piano.
Your brain goes, "Oh, there might be another one.
Keep going."
And here's the twist: dopamine isn't really your "pleasure" chemical.
Research suggests it's more about wanting.
The chase.
Not the catch.
Which is why you can scroll for an hour, enjoy almost none of it, and still not stop.
Now hold on, because trick number two was invented by one guy in 2006, and today, he says he regrets it.
And trick number three?
You do it with your thumb, probably twenty minutes ago.
Trick number two: no bottom.
Remember when websites had pages?
Page one, page two, "next"?
Every "next" was a tiny moment where your brain asked, "Do I actually want more?"
In 2006, a designer named Aza Raskin built infinite scroll to make browsing smoother.
No more pages.
No more pauses.
And he's since said he didn't see how it would be used to capture people's attention.
No bottom means no stopping point.
And without a stopping point, your brain just never gets the memo.
It's like a bag of chips that refills itself.
Which, honestly, I would buy.
That's the problem.
Trick number three: the lever.
That little pull-down-to-refresh gesture?
It first showed up in a Twitter app called Tweetie.
Critics, including former Google design ethicist Tristan Harris, have compared it to pulling the lever on a slot machine.
Pull.
Wait.
Spin.
New stuff?
Sometimes.
Which, as we just learned, is the most addictive kind of "sometimes."
So how do you fight back?
Three moves.
One: build your own stopping point.
Set a timer before you open the app, not after.
Two: add friction.
Move your most addictive app off your home screen, or log out after every use.
Every extra tap is a tiny "are you sure?"
Three: make your phone less shiny.
Try grayscale mode for a day.
A gray dog playing piano just hits different.
You didn't fail.
You were outplayed.
