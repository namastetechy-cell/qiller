You'd never call a 30-year-old tree a failure.
So why do you feel like you're losing at 30?
Nobody tells you the real reason.
Let me tell you about Mike.
Mike is turning 30 tonight.
His friends are inside the restaurant, waiting with a cake.
And Mike?
He's sitting in his car in the parking lot, scrolling.
Old college roommate: just bought a house.
Girl from his first job: engaged, in Italy, holding a ring the size of a golf ball.
A guy he used to beat at Mario Kart: "Thrilled to announce I'm now a VP."
And Mike looks at his own life.
Decent job.
Small apartment.
A plant he's kept alive for three whole weeks.
And he thinks: "I'm behind."
Here's what nobody told Mike.
In 1954, a psychologist named Leon Festinger described something called social comparison.
We figure out how we're doing by comparing ourselves to other people.
That's normal.
That's human.
But here's the problem.
Mike isn't comparing his life to their lives.
He's comparing his behind-the-scenes to their highlight reel.
Nobody posts "Sat in my car and cried a little."
Nobody announces "Still renting.
Doing fine."
So Mike loses that game every single time.
Not because he's failing.
Because the game is rigged.
And that's only the first half.
Because there's a second reason you feel like a failure at 30.
It's a deadline.
And here's the twist: nobody actually wrote it.
Think about it.
Who decided you needed the house, the spouse, and the corner office by 30?
There's no law.
No official rulebook.
It's just a timeline we all quietly agreed to, then forgot we made up.
And real life?
It ignores that timeline all the time.
Julia Child published her first cookbook at 49.
Vera Wang started designing wedding dresses at 40.
Colonel Sanders started franchising his chicken in his 60s.
If any of them had quit at 30 because they felt "behind," the world would have less butter, fewer gowns, and way less fried chicken.
And honestly?
That's a tragedy.
So back to Mike in the parking lot.
He puts the phone down.
He walks inside.
His friends yell "Surprise!" even though he saw them through the window ten minutes ago.
He laughs.
He eats too much cake.
And for one night, he's not behind anyone.
Because at 30, you're not late.
You're just early in a story that's longer than you think.
Oh, and Mike's plant?
Died the next morning.
Some timelines are real.
Here's the lesson: stop comparing your chapter 3 to someone else's chapter 20.
And if this helped even a little, subscribe to Mojo Mike Lessons.
We're right on time.
